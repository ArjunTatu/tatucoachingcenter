Q1. What is degeneracy in transportation problem? How is it resolved?

In a transportation problem, we try to allocate supply to demand in such a way that the total transportation cost becomes minimum.
During this process, we create a basic feasible solution (BFS) using methods like NWCR, LCM, or VAM.

Definition of Degeneracy:- The number of occupied cells (allocations) in the basic feasible solution is less than (m + n – 1).

Where:

·        m = number of supply points (sources)

·        n = number of demand points (destinations)

For an ideal, non-degenerate solution: Number of occupied (basic) cells = m + n – 1

Why does degeneracy happen?

·        A row or column becomes exhausted too early.

·        Two or more allocations tie for minimum cost.

·        While applying VAM or LCM, an allocation of “0” remains uncounted.

·        Supply and demand values are equal and eliminate each other in a single step.

Problem with Degeneracy

·        We cannot proceed to MODI or Stepping Stone method.

·        It breaks the rule of m + n – 1 basic variables.

·        The improvement process becomes blocked or gives infinite solutions.

So, degeneracy must be removed before optimization.

How to Resolve Degeneracy?

We resolve degeneracy using the ε (epsilon) method.

Q2. What is unbalanced transportation problem? How is it solved?

Unbalanced Transportation Problem (Definition)

Total Supply ≠ Total Demand

In a normal (balanced) transportation problem: Total supply = Total demand
So all goods supplied from sources are exactly equal to goods required at destinations.

Why does unbalance occur?

·        Companies have extra production than required.

·        Customer demand is more than available supply.

·        Certain sources or destinations are added or removed.

·        Forecasting errors create mismatch.

Types of Unbalanced Transportation Problems

1. Supply > Demand

Total supply is more than total demand.
This means after meeting all demands, some goods are left unused.

2. Demand > Supply

Total demand is more than total supply.
This means all supply will be used, but some demand remains unmet.

Problems Due to Unbalance

·        Cannot be solved directly using NWCR, VAM, LCM, MODI, etc.

·        Causes calculation errors.

·        Makes loops and BFS invalid.

So we must balance the table first, then start the solution.

How to Solve an Unbalanced Transportation Problem?

The standard method is: Add a Dummy Row or Dummy Column

This is done to make: Total supply = Total demand

Q5. Explain steps involved in solving transportation problem using least cost Method

LCM is a technique to find an initial basic feasible solution (IBFS) for a transportation problem. As the name suggests, we start allocating from the lowest transportation cost cell to reduce the overall cost.

Steps to Solve a Transportation Problem Using Least Cost Method (LCM)

Step 1: Prepare the Cost Table

·        List all sources (rows), destinations (columns), supply, and demand.

·        Check if the problem is balanced (total supply = total demand).

·        If not balanced, add a dummy row or column with zero cost.

Step 2: Identify the Cell With the Least Cost

·        In the entire cost matrix, find the minimum cost value.

·        If minimum cost appears in more than one cell, select any one of them.

Step 3: Allocate as Much as Possible

·        Compare the supply of that row and demand of that column.

·        Allocate the minimum of the two values to that cell

Step 4: Adjust Supply and Demand

After allocation:

·        If supply becomes zero, cross out the row.

·        If demand becomes zero, cross out the column.

·        If both supply and demand become zero at the same time, cross out only one, and leave the other with zero (to avoid degeneracy).

Step 5: Repeat for the Next Least Cost Cell

·        From the remaining un-crossed cells, find the next lowest cost.

·        Allocate again using the same rule.

·        Continue until all supply and demand are satisfied and all rows/columns are crossed out.

Step 6: Check Number of Allocations

·        The number of allocated (occupied) cells must be m + n – 1.

·        If less, the solution is degenerate, and we add a very small value (ε) in a suitable cell.

Step 7: Initial Basic Feasible Solution (IBFS) Obtained

·        Once all demands and supplies are allocated fully, the LCM table is complete.

·        Now this can be improved using MODI or Stepping Stone method to find the optimal solution.

Advantages of Least Cost Method

·        Faster and simpler compared to VAM.

·        Gives better initial solution than NWCR.

·        Reduces initial cost by starting from the lowest values.

Q7. Explain the following in the context of assignment problem

·        The Hungarian Method

·        Infesible assignment

i) The Hungarian Method (in Assignment Problem)

The Hungarian Method is a systematic and efficient algorithm used to solve the assignment problem. Its objective is to assign jobs to persons at minimum cost or maximum efficiency.

This method works only when the cost matrix is square (number of rows = number of columns).
If it is not square, we convert it into a square matrix by adding a dummy row/column.

Steps in the Hungarian Method

Step 1: Row Reduction

Subtract the minimum value of each row from all elements of that row.
This ensures at least one zero in every row.

Step 2: Column Reduction

Subtract the minimum value of each column from all elements of that column.
Now each column will also have at least one zero.

Step 3: Cover All Zeros Using Minimum Number of Lines

Cover all zeros using the least possible horizontal and vertical lines.

  • If number of lines = n (matrix size), we have an optimal assignment.
  • If number of lines < n, go to next step.

Step 4: Create Additional Zeros

Find the smallest uncovered element, subtract it from all uncovered cells, and add it to the intersections of the lines.
This step creates new zeros.

Step 5: Make the Assignments

Choose zeros such that:

  • Only one assignment per row
  • Only one assignment per column

This gives the optimal (minimum cost) assignment.

Why is the Hungarian Method Important?

  • It always gives an optimal solution.
  • It is easy and fast to use.
  • Suitable for both cost minimization and profit maximization.
  • It avoids trial-and-error used in earlier methods.

ii) Infeasible Assignment (in Assignment Problem)

An infeasible assignment is a situation where a particular job cannot be assigned to a particular person, even though it appears in the matrix.

Why does an infeasible assignment occur?

Because:

  • A worker may not have the required skill
  • A machine cannot perform a certain job
  • A subcontractor is not allowed to take up some task
  • Some tasks are restricted due to job rules or safety reasons
  • Certain combinations are not permitted in real life

How is an Infeasible Assignment Represented?

In the cost matrix:

  • We put a very high cost (M) or
  • Write ∞ (infinity)
  • Or simply a dash (-)

This indicates that the assignment is not allowed.

The Hungarian method will naturally avoid selecting that cell because:

  • It has a very high cost
  • It will never become a zero
  • It cannot be chosen during assignment

Q9. Explain steps involved in VAM

Vogel’s Approximation Method (VAM) : VAM is one of the best methods to obtain an initial solution for a transportation problem because it usually gives a result very close to the optimal cost. It works by calculating a penalty for each row and column to understand where a wrong allocation would cost the most.

Step 1: Check if the Transportation Problem is Balanced

  • Add total supply and total demand.
  • If they are unequal, add a dummy row or column with zero cost to balance the table.

Step 2: Calculate Row and Column Penalties

For each row and column:

  • Identify the two smallest costs.
  • Subtract the smallest from the second smallest.

This difference is called the penalty.

Penalty = (2nd minimum cost) – (minimum cost)

The penalty tells how much we lose if we do not choose the best option in that row/column.

Step 3: Select the Highest Penalty

  • From all row and column penalties, pick the largest penalty.
  • This shows the row or column where choosing the wrong cell is most costly.

Step 4: Allocate to the Lowest-Cost Cell in That Row/Column

  • In the selected row/column (with highest penalty), find the lowest cost cell.
  • Allocate as much as possible:

Step 5: Adjust Supply and Demand

After allocation:

  • If supply becomes zero → cross out that row.
  • If demand becomes zero → cross out that column.
  • If both become zero → cross out only one and leave zero in the other to avoid degeneracy.

Step 6: Recalculate Penalties

  • After crossing out the completed row/column,
  • Recalculate penalties for the remaining active rows and columns.

Step 7: Repeat the Process

Continue:

  1. Calculate penalties
  2. Pick highest penalty
  3. Allocate to the least cost cell

Repeat until all supplies and demands are fully allocated.

Step 8: IBFS Obtained

When all the rows and columns are crossed out, the VAM table is completed.

You now have the Initial Basic Feasible Solution (usually quite close to optimal).

Q10. What is meant by unbalanced assignment problem? State the steps to solve such problem

Unbalanced Assignment Problem

An assignment problem deals with assigning n jobs to n persons (or machines) in the most efficient way, usually minimizing cost or maximizing profit.

Normally, the assignment problem is balanced, meaning:

  • Number of jobs = Number of persons
    (i.e., square cost matrix)

But sometimes, in practical situations:

  • Number of jobs ≠ number of persons

This type of assignment problem is called an:

Unbalanced Assignment Problem

Reasons for unbalance:

  • More workers than tasks
  • More tasks than workers
  • Some machines may be idle
  • Extra jobs that cannot be assigned directly

To solve the assignment using the Hungarian Method, the matrix must be square.
So, we first convert it into a square matrix.

Steps to Solve an Unbalanced Assignment Problem

Step 1 — Check if the matrix is balanced

  • Count number of rows (persons)
  • Count number of columns (jobs)

If rows ≠ columns → it is an unbalanced problem.

Step 2 — Add Dummy Row or Dummy Column

  • If persons > jobs, add a dummy column
    → represents an imaginary job
  • If jobs > persons, add a dummy row
    → represents an imaginary person

Cost for dummy row/column = 0

Because assigning something to a dummy variable represents:

  • idle person
  • unassigned job
  • no actual cost

Step 3 — Solve the new square matrix

Once balanced by adding dummy rows/columns, use the Hungarian Method:

  1. Row reduction
  2. Column reduction
  3. Draw minimum number of lines
  4. Adjust uncovered elements
  5. Find optimal assignment

Step 4 — Interpret the solution

  • If assignment is made to a dummy, it means:
    → That worker remains idle
    → That job is unassigned

The remaining assignments give the optimal real solution.

Q1. Write the rules for constructing the Dual programming.

Rules for Constructing the Dual

  1. Change the objective type:
    If the primal is a maximization problem, the dual becomes a minimization problem, and if the primal is minimization, the dual becomes maximization.
  2. Exchange variables and constraints:
    The number of primal constraints becomes the number of dual variables, and the number of primal variables becomes the number of dual constraints.
  3. Transpose the coefficient matrix:
    The coefficients in the primal constraints are written in transpose form when forming the dual.
  4. Swap RHS and objective coefficients:
    The right-hand side (RHS) values of the primal constraints become the objective function coefficients of the dual.
    The objective coefficients of the primal become the RHS in the dual constraints.
  5. Dual of dual is primal:
    If you construct the dual of the dual, you get back the original primal problem.

Q3. Explain following terms:

                 i.          Slack variables

               ii.          Surplus variable

             iii.          Artificial variable

i) Slack Variable

A slack variable is a variable added to a ≤ constraint to convert it into an equation.

It represents the unused or leftover capacity in the constraint. Slack variables are always non-negative.

Example:
If ( x1 + x2 ≤ 10 ), add slack variable ( s1 ):
( x1 + x2 + s1 = 10 ).

ii) Surplus Variable

A surplus variable is a variable subtracted from a ≥ constraint to convert it into an equation.

It represents the excess or extra amount over the minimum requirement. Surplus variables are also non-negative.

Example:
If ( x1 + x2 ≥ 20 ), subtract surplus variable ( s1 ):
( x1 + x2 - s1 = 20 ).

iii) Artificial Variable

An artificial variable is a temporary variable added to ≥ or = constraints to obtain an initial basic feasible solution for the simplex method.

Artificial variables help start the solution process but must be removed later using Big-M or Two-Phase methods because they have no physical meaning.

Example:
For a constraint ( x1 + x2 - s1 = 20 ), add artificial variable ( A1 ):
( x1 + x2 - s1 + A1 = 20 ).

Q6. What the sensitivity analysis is as applied in Linear programming problem? Explain with the help of example

Sensitivity Analysis in Linear Programming

Sensitivity analysis (also called post-optimality analysis) studies how the optimal solution of a Linear Programming Problem changes when the values in the LPP—such as objective coefficients, RHS values, or constraint coefficients—are changed slightly.

In simple words, sensitivity analysis tells us:

  • How sensitive the optimal solution is to changes in profits, costs, or resource availability.
  • Whether the current solution will remain optimal if these values change.
  • Up to what limit (range) the coefficients can change without changing the optimal basis.

It helps managers know which resources are more valuable, which constraints are critical, and how much variation is allowed before the optimal solution changes.

1. Change in Objective Coefficient

If profit of product ( x1 ) increases from 3 to 4:

The optimal solution remains same, only profit changes.
This means the coefficient of ( x1 ) can increase up to its “allowable range” without changing the basic solution.

2. Change in RHS Values (Resources)

If available material increases from 100 to 110,
the constraint becomes:

This may improve the feasible region and give a new optimal solution, because more material allows the company to produce more units.

Thus, sensitivity analysis tells:

  • Is the old solution still optimal?
  • How much will the profit increase?
  • Which constraint is binding (tight)?

3. Identifying Important Constraints

If labour constraint is tight (no slack), small changes in its RHS affect the solution heavily.

Such constraints are critical, and their dual values (shadow prices) show how valuable the resource is.

Q7. In Simplex problem, explain the two phase method and its applications

Two-Phase Method

The Two-Phase Method is a technique used in the Simplex method to solve Linear Programming Problems that involve ≥ constraints, = constraints, or cases where an initial basic feasible solution (IBFS) is not available.

This method removes artificial variables in a systematic way and helps start the simplex algorithm properly.

Phase – 1

  • In this phase, artificial variables are added to those constraints where no obvious basic variables exist.
  • A temporary objective function is formed by minimizing the sum of all artificial variables.
  • The Simplex method is applied to make all artificial variables zero.
  • If the minimum value becomes zero, a feasible solution for the original problem is found.

Phase – 2

  • Once artificial variables are removed, the original objective function is restored.
  • The Simplex method is now applied normally to find the optimal solution of the actual LPP.

Applications of Two-Phase Method

  1. Used when no natural basic feasible solution exists:
    This happens in problems with ≥ or = type constraints, where slack variables do not provide an initial solution.
  2. Used when artificial variables are necessary:
    In some constraints, artificial variables must be introduced; the two-phase method helps remove them without using a large number like M.
  3. Preferred in large and complex problems:
    Two-phase method is more stable and avoids numerical problems caused by the Big-M method.
  4. Used in problems with equality constraints:
    Constraints like ( a1x1 + a2x2 = b ) need artificial variables, so phase-1 is applied.
  5. Used in computer-based simplex solvers:
    Software and optimization tools prefer two-phase because it is more reliable and accurate.

Q9. Define the following terms and indicate their significance of decision making with linear programming and simplex method:

                           I.          Key cloumn

                         II.          Key row

i) Key Column (Pivot Column)

Definition:
The key column is the column in the simplex table that indicates which non-basic variable will enter the basis.
It is selected from the objective function row (Cj – Zj row) by choosing the most positive value in a maximization problem or the most negative value in a minimization problem.

Significance in Decision Making:

  • Shows which variable should increase to improve the objective value (profit or cost).
  • Helps decide the direction of movement toward optimality.
  • Ensures that each iteration moves toward a better solution.

ii) Key Row (Pivot Row)

Definition:
The key row is the row selected to identify which basic variable will leave the basis.
It is chosen by taking the minimum positive ratio of (RHS ÷ key column values).

Significance in Decision Making:

  • Determines how much the entering variable can increase before violating any constraint.
  • Prevents infeasible solutions by keeping all values non-negative.
  • Ensures the algorithm moves within the feasible region.

Circumstances When Big M Method Is Used

The Big M method is used in situations where an initial basic feasible solution (IBFS) is not available in a Linear Programming Problem.
This mainly occurs in the following cases:

  1. When the LPP has ≥ (greater-than-or-equal-to) type constraints
    Because these constraints need a surplus variable and an artificial variable, and slack variables cannot give a feasible starting solution.
  2. When the LPP has = (equality) type constraints
    Equality constraints do not provide slack variables, so artificial variables are required to start the simplex method.
  3. When artificial variables are necessary to start the solution
    Big M method assigns a large penalty (M) to these artificial variables so that they are forced out of the final solution.
  4. When we want to solve the problem in one phase
    Unlike the Two-Phase method, Big M solves the problem in a single simplex phase, making it useful for smaller problems.

Q2. What are the similarities between Golden section and Fibonacci method?

✅ Similarities Between Golden Section Method & Fibonacci Method

1. Both are used for Unimodal Functions

  • Both methods work only when the function has a single minimum in the given interval (unimodal function).
  • They progressively reduce the interval that contains the minimum.

2. Both are Interval-Reduction Methods

  • They do not use derivatives.
  • They locate the minimum by repeatedly shrinking the search interval.

3. Both evaluate the function at two interior points

  • In each iteration, two interior points inside the interval are selected.
  • Depending on function values, one part of the interval is removed.

4. No Need of Derivative (Derivative-Free Methods)

  • Both methods only require function values, not slope or derivative.
  • Useful when function is noisy, discontinuous, or derivative is difficult.

5. Based on Ratio / Sequence Patterns

  • Golden section uses constant golden ratio (0.618).
  • Fibonacci method uses Fibonacci sequence, which approaches the golden ratio.
  • Both rely on a structured mathematical pattern for choosing interior points.

6. Only One Function Evaluation per Iteration (after first step)

  • In both methods, one of the interior points is reused in the next iteration.
  • This saves computational time and reduces calculations.

7. Guarantee Convergence

  • Both methods guarantee convergence to the minimum as the interval shrinks.
  • Fibonacci method gives exact number of iterations in advance.
  • Golden section gives approximate number, but still converges.

8. Both are Bracketing Techniques

  • They do not jump randomly.
  • They trap the minimum between two boundaries and repeatedly narrow it.

9. Both Use a Similar Strategy

  • Compare the function values at two points.
  • Choose the sub-interval where the minimum lies.
  • Discard the other sub-interval.

Hessian Matrix and Gradient Vector

In optimization of multivariable functions, especially in methods like Newton’s method, steepest descent, and steepest ascent, two important mathematical tools are used: the Gradient Vector and the Hessian Matrix. Both describe how a function changes with respect to its variables and help in locating minima or maxima.

(i) Hessian Matrix

Definition

The Hessian Matrix is a square matrix made up of all second-order partial derivatives of a multivariable function.

Meaning / Interpretation

  • The Hessian matrix gives information about the curvature of the function.
  • If the Hessian is positive definite at a point → the function has a local minimum.
  • If the Hessian is negative definite → the function has a local maximum.
  • If the Hessian is indefinite → the point is a saddle point (neither min nor max).

Importance

  • Used in Newton’s method and Modified Newton’s method for faster convergence.
  • Helps understand how the function bends in different directions.
  • Necessary for solving non-linear optimization problems in engineering.
  • Indicates how the value of the function accelerates or decelerates in any direction.

(ii) Gradient Vector

Definition

The Gradient Vector of a multivariable function is the vector of all first-order partial derivatives.

Meaning / Interpretation

  • The gradient gives the direction of maximum increase of the function.
  • Its negative gives the direction of maximum decrease (used in steepest descent).
  • The magnitude of the gradient shows how fast the function changes.

Importance

  • Required in steepest ascent and steepest descent optimization methods.
  • Helps in iterative movement towards optimum solution.
  • Commonly used in civil engineering optimization:
    • structural optimization
    • slope stability
    • signal timing
    • pavement design

  • The Gradient Vector tells us direction and rate of change of a function using first derivatives.
  • The Hessian Matrix tells us curvature and nature of the point using second derivatives.
    Together, they form the mathematical foundation for most optimization techniques used in civil engineering, mathematics, and machine learning.

Q1. What are the characteristics of Game theory?

Characteristics of Game Theory

Game theory studies decision-making in situations where two or more players interact, and the outcome for each player depends not only on their own actions but also on the actions of others. The key characteristics are:

1. Two or More Decision Makers (Players)

A game always involves multiple players, such as individuals, groups, firms, or countries, who make strategic decisions.

2. Conflicting Interests

Players usually have opposing objectives. One player’s gain is often another player’s loss, especially in zero-sum games.

3. Set of Strategies

Each player has a defined set of possible actions or strategies from which they can choose.

4. Payoff or Outcome

Every combination of strategies results in a specific payoff (profit, cost, reward, loss) for each player.

5. Rational Behaviour

All players are assumed to behave rationally—they aim to maximize their own payoff or minimize loss.

6. Interdependence of Decisions

The outcome for each player depends on the combined decisions of all players. No player can decide independently.

7. Rules of the Game

Games operate under a defined set of rules, such as allowed strategies, sequence of moves, and how payoffs are determined.

8. Types of Games

Game theory includes:

  • Pure strategy & mixed strategy games
  • Zero-sum & non-zero-sum games
  • Cooperative & non-cooperative games
  • Two-person & multi-person games

Q3. Why is replacement of items required? what is the strategy used for replacement of any machine? What are the types of costs to be considered for calculating the replacement year?

Replacement of Items

Replacement theory deals with the decision of when an old machine or equipment should be replaced by a new one in order to minimize cost and maintain efficiency. In civil engineering and construction projects, machinery like mixers, vibrators, pumps, generators, and vehicles gradually become inefficient due to age, wear, and high maintenance.

1) Why is Replacement of Items Required?

Replacement is required mainly due to:

a) Physical Deterioration

Machines lose efficiency with age due to wear and tear.
Fuel consumption increases, output decreases, and breakdowns become frequent.

b) Functional Obsolescence

Newer technology becomes available which is faster, safer, and cheaper to operate.
Old machines may not meet modern project requirements.

c) Increase in Maintenance Cost

As machines age, the cost of servicing, repairing, and replacing parts increases per year.

d) Increase in Operating Cost

Older equipment uses more fuel, electricity, or labour.
Productivity reduces, leading to higher cost per unit of work.

e) Safety and Reliability Issues

Old machines may malfunction and cause accidents at construction sites.

f) Economic Reasons

To minimize the total cost (capital + maintenance + operating), replacement becomes necessary at the economically optimum time.

2) Strategy Used for Replacement of Any Machine

The general strategy is based on minimizing the average annual cost or maximizing efficiency. The steps in replacement strategy are:

Step 1: Determine all relevant costs

Initial cost, maintenance cost, operating cost, resale value, etc., are collected for each year of the machine’s life.

Step 2: Calculate Annual Operating & Maintenance Costs

Prepare a year-wise table showing increasing maintenance and operating costs.

Step 3: Add Capital Recovery Cost

Include depreciation and interest to find annual capital cost.

Step 4: Compute Total Annual Cost

Total Annual Cost = Operating Cost + Maintenance Cost + Capital Cost – Salvage Value/Benefit

Step 5: Compare Costs for Each Year

Calculate average yearly cost for 1 year, 2 years, 3 years… up to the probable life of the machine.

Step 6: Select Minimum Average Cost Year

The year in which average annual cost is minimum is considered the optimal replacement year.

This method ensures the machine is replaced before costs become unnecessarily high.

3) Types of Costs to be Considered in Determining Replacement Year

For replacement analysis, the following costs must be included:

a) Capital Cost

  • Purchase cost of new machine
  • Installation and commissioning charges
    Capital cost reduces each year due to depreciation.

b) Operating Cost

  • Fuel, power consumption
  • Labour, operating wages
    This cost increases with age as machine efficiency decreases.

c) Maintenance Cost

  • Repairs, servicing, replacement of parts
  • Downtime losses
    This cost increases significantly every year.

d) Depreciation Cost

  • Reduction in machine value due to use and age
    It is part of the capital recovery cost.

e) Salvage or Resale Value

  • The value at which the old machine can be sold.
  • Deducted from total cost or added as a benefit.
